01 / THE PROBLEM

THE GAP BETWEEN DATA AND CARE

The platform sees the pattern.
Who sees the person?

Imagine teaching 120 students. One student who always submits early begins turning work in at 11:59 p.m. Then their scores decline and deadlines are missed. Each change is small. Together, they might matter.

A 2024 mixed-methods study from one university in Kazakhstan contains precisely this institutional concern: interviewees described high faculty workload and difficulty keeping track of students before they reached academic probation. This is case-specific qualitative evidence—not a measured national frequency of missed warning signs. [1]

LOCAL RESEARCH EXISTS

But the key question remains open.

Sergazina's NU thesis examined academic probation using 7,000+ student records; Daurenbayeva's qualitative research explores reasons students persist. A Kazakhstan university study of 576 students also considers well-being and academic achievement. In Sergazina's interviews, one program's student–faculty ratio was described as about 54:1, and administrators raised concerns about students being noticed only after serious academic difficulties. These are perspectives from one institution, not representative estimates. None of the studies tests personalized Baiqa alerts. [1–3]

Local theses are not peer-reviewed journal articles; the available Daurenbayeva material is the public abstract.

The scale of the setting, not the scale of the problem. The Bureau of National Statistics counts 116 higher education institutions and 678.1 thousand enrolled students. These totals describe the potential institutional context; they do not reveal how often faculty miss academic disruptions or whether large classes cause them. [22]

What is changing over time? Digital academic records make new kinds of monitoring possible, but we found no nationwide time series measuring missed support opportunities; the qualitative evidence from one university cannot answer how frequent they are nationally. 2. Transfer is difficult. Models evaluated on a later year performed differently; apparent accuracy is not a portable guarantee for Kazakhstan.

THE NEGATIVE RESULT MATTERS TOO

More alerts can mean more false alarms.

A separate real-OULAD study (Sánchez Trujillo et al., 2026) reported about 427 withdrawal-risk alerts per 1,000 enrolments at one selected threshold, including around 93 eventual withdrawals and 333 false positives (rounded counts). [25]

A “false positive” here means the predicted withdrawal did not occur; that student might still benefit from outreach. The threshold, population and outcome are not Baiqa's. These findings reveal a potential staff-capacity risk, not Baiqa's expected error rate.

DATA LANDSCAPE · THREE DIFFERENT MEASURES

What is counted—and who remains invisible?

National education statistics

BNS reports 678.1 thousand students enrolled across 116 institutions (2025–26). These totals locate the university system but say nothing about overlooked difficulties or intervention delays. [22]

Kazakhstan youth-health survey

MICS-derived screening estimates concern young people aged 15–24, including non-students. They do not link to Moodle records or prove that academic changes indicate distress. [4]

University and learning-platform records

Grades and activity can document patterns; availability, meanings and permissions differ by course and institution. The UK randomized trial tests messages, not Kazakhstan faculty-alert workflows. [9]

The missing denominator: the single-university thesis supplies an academic-probation rate and qualitative evidence of workload, but not a verified count of how many significant academic changes were missed by faculty, how long outreach took, or how many students benefited. That gap is an explicit constraint on the claim—not proof that the problem is widespread.

The disagreement matters. Predictive modeling papers ask “can we estimate risk?”; intervention studies ask “does acting help?”; methodological and ethics studies ask “what are we missing or risking?” Baiqa needs all three answers before implementation.
WHERE THE EVIDENCE ACTUALLY DISAGREES

Three tensions—not a list of papers.

Can a model predict risk?

Macfadyen & Dawson and Wu et al. demonstrate useful signals in LMS records, but Kovanović et al. show that activity measurements depend on methodological assumptions. Measured behavior is not time studying. [5] [24]

Does a better model improve decisions?

Sánchez Trujillo et al. found essentially identical later-cohort discrimination for logistic regression (0.7102) and random forest (0.7104). Hasan & Khan found benefits from messages, but tested student-directed forecasts—not professor review. [25] [9]

Does minimal data mean fair data?

Wu et al. found that dropping background features sacrificed some predictive performance and did not reliably eliminate subgroup disparities. Pardo & Siemens argue that transparency and student rights must govern analytics. Use less data where possible, then audit for unequal errors. [24] [18]

Research gap: None of these sources tests whether an opt-in, self-baseline alert shown to a professor improves appropriate outreach in Kazakhstan compared with a transparent rule. That is the outcome Baiqa must investigate, not assume.

THE RESEARCH GAP ✳

Can personalized change detection plus human-led outreach improve student support in Kazakhstan more than existing Moodle analytics or a simple set of transparent rules?

The answer is unknown. That is why Baiqa is a proposal for a controlled pilot, not an already-proven intervention.

04B / DOES IT ADD VALUE?

A TEST, NOT A TECH PROMISE

More signals ≠ better support.

Baiqa needs evidence against the simplest credible alternative. A professor's time and a student's privacy are not free just because the software runs quietly.

ARM 0

Existing support

Ordinary faculty observation and established academic-advising pathways; no new automated alert.

ARM 1

Simple rules

Transparent thresholds for missed work or substantial grade changes; minimal new analytics.

ARM 2

Baiqa extension

Validated multi-indicator, self-baseline change detection, with human-reviewed alerts and opt-in safeguards.

What we would measure

  • Timely, appropriate outreach—not just number of alerts
  • Precision, recall, missed events and alert burden
  • Time to review and staffing capacity
  • Students' experience, trust, opt-out and complaint rates
  • Follow-up support access, academic recovery and retention

What would make us stop?

  • No meaningful benefit above simple rules
  • Disproportionate false alerts for any student group
  • Privacy incidents, unapproved access or coercive outreach
  • Alert volume exceeds trained staff capacity
  • Students cannot freely decline participation

Proposed study design: Begin with de-identified retrospective testing, followed by a properly approved, informed opt-in pilot. Use consistent prediction windows, student-level data separation between training and testing, and course-context controls. Compare arms prospectively only if an ethics review approves the design. No real student records are present on this website.

Five dimensions. Five questions we cannot skip.

Click to examine each cost.

01 / ENVIRONMENTALIs a new model worth the energy?+

Prefer small, explainable models and existing servers. Estimate actual runtime, data-center overhead and embodied hardware footprint; compare with a rule-only system.

02 / ECONOMICWho pays when alerts create more work?+

Universities fund integration, testing, security, faculty training and follow-up. An alert that produces new counseling demand has a real staffing cost. Suppliers may earn development fees.

03 / LABORDo we lighten work—or move it to professors?+

Faculty review time, technical maintenance, advisor and counseling workload must be evaluated. False alerts can exhaust human attention; missed alerts can produce false reassurance.

04 / GOVERNANCEWho gets to watch the watchers?+

AI and personal-data law, institutional access permissions, oversight, appeals, security and limits on secondary use all matter. Human review is necessary but not sufficient for legal compliance.

05 / DATA ETHICSCan students freely say no?+

Opt-in, transparent data collection, limited retention, restricted access, no mental-health scoring, no disciplinary use, a challenge process and withdrawal without academic penalty are central safeguards.

06 / THE VERDICT

A conditional yes.
And a very clear not yet.

Test Baiqa on a small, voluntary basis. Do not roll it out across a campus until it improves meaningful student support compared with ordinary monitoring, simpler alerts and existing Moodle analytics. Recent real-data studies show that more complex models do not always improve on simpler models and that false alert volumes can be substantial; these findings strengthen the case for a comparison pilot, not a mandate.
